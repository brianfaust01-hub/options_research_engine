# Stonks in plain language

Stonks is a research and paper-trading system designed to find promising short-term stock moves and decide whether an options trade is a sensible way to act on them. It starts with a broad stock universe and applies a technical pipeline that looks at trend, momentum, price behavior and market conditions. This produces bullish and bearish opportunities, with scores that help compare their strength. Those scores are research signals, not proven probabilities of making money.

The system then looks for suitable call or put contracts and puts them through additional checks. It considers expiration, price, trading activity, the gap between buying and selling prices, execution quality and earnings timing. It favors longer-dated options to give a shorter trading thesis more time to work without relying on contracts close to expiration. A promising stock idea can still be rejected if the available options are too expensive to trade efficiently or fail the other checks.

The surviving contracts compete for capital alongside positions already held. Stonks ranks the opportunities and considers available capital, position size, planned loss and concentration across related investments. The output is a smaller set of actionable recommendations: the exact contract, recommended number of contracts, entry limit, profit target, stop level and expected holding period. It also reviews existing positions and recommends holding, adding, reducing or closing them. Qualified ideas can remain unfunded, and cash can be the appropriate result. Orders are currently entered manually in the broker's paper-trading account.

Stonks keeps the original recommendations, broker statements and available option-price observations so we can compare what it expected with what happened. Hindsight asks separate questions: did the stock move as anticipated, did the option make money, and did selection, sizing, timing or execution help or hurt? Ideas that were not traded remain useful research evidence. Actual account results stay separate from simulated outcomes and explicitly reviewed execution adjustments, so learning does not require rewriting history.

The goal is to develop an explainable process that can produce sustained returns and adapt as market conditions change. We are testing that process in a twelve-week paper experiment rather than assuming that an encouraging run proves it works. Proposed changes are evaluated against the existing approach before adoption. The prepared next comparison will test a moderate-delta and moderate-IV entry filter in a separate simulated portfolio, using recorded option quotes to model entries and exits. As evidence accumulates, we can assess which signals, contracts, exit rules and potentially which stocks deserve more focus.
